## How do I tell if a Hypixel SkyBlock flip has a real profit after fees and enough trading volume to sell?

Use **net profit = gross completed-sale receipts − acquisition cost of sold units − charges not already deducted**. If your recorded receipt is already net of a fee, do not subtract that fee twice. Check the charges shown for your transaction rather than assuming a universal tax rate.

The [dated Bazaar example](/guides/bazaar-flipping-guide-2026) quotes a 49,900-coin immediate purchase and a 46,140-coin immediate exit for 100 Enchanted Carrots on 29 October 2026 at 14:36 UTC: **−3,760 coins before separate charges**. Adding fees cannot turn that loss into profit. This is quote arithmetic, not a completed trade.

Check how much of your own trial order actually fills and sells during your observation window. Market-wide volume and available supply are context, not a guarantee that your quantity will clear. Stop scaling when your own exit stalls or the current net exit no longer covers cost.

## How can I track my actual Hypixel SkyBlock flipping profits and decide whether crafting or Bazaar flipping is working better?

Keep a ledger outside NEC; NEC teaches the accounting and does not provide a profit tracker. Use one row per batch and compare equal observation periods. Here is a worksheet for two sessions; the blanks require your actual transaction records, not a price quote.

| Field | Bazaar | Craft |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date and active minutes | Record start/end | Record start/end |
| Cost of units sold | Actual filled purchase costs | All inputs used for sold output |
| Gross completed-sale receipts | Actual sales only | Actual output sales only |
| Separate fees and charges | Record once | Record once |
| Realized profit | Receipts minus sold cost minus charges | Receipts minus sold cost minus charges |
| Unsold inventory at cost | Keep outside realized profit | Include unused inputs and unsold crafts |
| Open commitments | Unfilled buy orders | Unfilled input orders |

Only compare profit per active minute once you also record elapsed time, capital tied up and unsold inventory. A profitable sold batch can coexist with losing inventory. The [craft quote example](/guides/craft-flipping-guide-2026) is a pre-purchase check; it cannot fill the completed-sales column.

## Margin is the beginning, not the answer

A high margin looks attractive because it compresses the story into one number. Unfortunately, that number is often the least important part of the decision. If the item barely sells, the spread may never become realized profit. If the market is thin, your own listing can become the problem. If competition is fierce, the margin may evaporate during the time it takes to exit. All of these issues are normal, and they are exactly why experienced flippers care so much about liquidity and turnover.

This is also why screenshot culture is misleading. A picture of one large spread says nothing about the exit. Good market reading demands a second question every time: who is actually buying this, how often and at what size? Without that answer, the margin is just theater.

## Volume changes what a good flip looks like

Volume is not glamorous, but it is one of the cleanest quality filters available. High-volume items often carry smaller spreads, yet those spreads can be far more valuable because the capital cycles reliably. Low-volume items can look spectacular and still trap your coins for hours or days. This is where the creator advice across different flipping styles converges. Whether the subject is bazaar, craft or auction flips, the best guides repeatedly warn players away from fake margin and toward repeatable turnover.

| Signal | High margin, low volume | Lower margin, high volume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital cycling | Slow, coins get stuck | Fast, capital reused repeatedly |
| Exit risk | High, your own listing can crash the price | Low, buyers absorb the item quickly |
| Real coins/hour | Often overstated by the screenshot | Often understated until you run it |
| Best for | Occasional patient flips | Repeatable, scalable routes |

## Risk is what remains after the screenshot ends

Risk includes more than price movement. It includes saturation, relist friction, tax drag, event exposure and the chance that you misunderstood the item's real buyer pool. In other words, risk is everything that makes the flip harder than the screenshot implied. This is why strong market players often sound less excited than new flippers. They are not pessimistic. They are simply aware of the hidden costs that determine whether a flip scales or breaks.
